\section{introduction}
Physics-informed neural computations have their theoretical 
underpinnings in the universal approximation theorem formulated 
by Hornik et al. \cite{hornikMultilayerFeedforwardNetworks1989}. 
The paper demonstrated that multilayer feedforward neural networks can 
approximate classes of continuous and measurable functions with
arbitrary accuracy. This theoretical result is of great importance
because it gave rise to the idea of representing unknown physical 
fields using the neural networks and, consequently, the solution of 
differential equations.

In the 1990s, efforts to combine neural networks with physical 
knowledge and differential equation modelling began. An early example was 
was proposed by Psichogios and Ungar \cite{psichogiosHybridNeuralNetworkfirst1992} when they presented 
the hybrid neural network-first-principles approach for process modeling. 
Here, the neural network served as a tool to approximate the unknown 
process parameters, whereas the known part of the system was modeled 
through physical relations like mass and energy balances. Even though 
the neural network was not used to approximate the solution of the
differential equation in this case, this was one of the first 
attempts to incorporate prior physical knowledge into the neural 
network models.

Building on the universal approximation theorem proposed by Hornik et al. \cite{hornikMultilayerFeedforwardNetworks1989},
the unknown solution of the PDE was approximated by a multi-layered feed-forward 
neural network as suggested by Dissanayake and Phan-Thien \cite{dissanayakeNeuralnetworkbasedApproximationsSolving1994}. The parameters 
of the network were adjusted such that the objective function made up of the residual 
from the governing-equation and boundary conditions at certain collocation points was minimized.
Given the similarity of the formulation of the objective function with those of 
current PINNs, the method suggested by Dissanayake and Phan-Thien might be regarded 
as an important precursor to modern physics-informed neural networks, even though physics-informed neural networks 
had not been invented yet.

Later in the decade, Lagaris et al. \cite{lagarisArtificialNeuralNetworks1998} proposed a framework for solving 
initial/boundary value problems defined by both ODEs and PDEs. 
Their framework included constructing the trial solutions through 
a known part satisfying the initial/boundary conditions and a variable part 
obtained by a neural network.


During the 2000s and early 2010s, improvements in computational power and numerical optimization 
led to the creation of highly developed techniques of solving differential equations using neural-network methods. 
As reviewed by Kumar and Yadav \cite{kumarMultilayerPerceptronsRadial2011} , a wide range of methods were proposed based on 
multilayer perceptron, radial-basis-function, hybrid,
and collocation methods of modeling ODEs and PDEs. The emergence of deep learning further increased 
the capacity of neural networks to perform complex computations, allowing researchers to train models consisting of multiple 
processing layers 
and therefore learn more complicated functions \cite{lecunDeepLearning2015}. Moreover, the rise of software platforms, such as 
TensorFlow and Keras made 
building and differentiating deep neural networks easier, faster and more convenient \cite{abadi2016tensorflow, ketkar2017introduction}. 
The first one provides a computation 
graph and automatic differentiation, making neural networks scalable across CPUs and GPUs, while the second allows defining and 
training the network architecture in a straightforward way.


In parallel with the above progress, researchers also investigated the use of deep learning for structured scientific problems.
For instance, Kondor and Trivedi \cite{kondor2018generalization} presented a theory on how symmetry/equivariance can be considered 
within neural network
frameworks, while Vlachas et al. \cite{vlachasDatadrivenForecastingHighdimensional2018} showed that LSTM networks can predict 
chaotic high-dimensional dynamical systems 
based solely on the input data. These works were not directly related to physics-informed neural networks,
but they made significant contribution to the progress in deep-learning techniques for modelling physical and dynamical systems.
Building upon earlier neural-network approaches for differential equations, advances in deep learning and automatic differentiation, 
and the growing integration of physical knowledge into machine learning, Raissi et al. 
\cite{raissiPhysicsinformedNeuralNetworks2019} developed the modern PINN formulation for nonlinear PDEs.

Following the work of Raissi , Haghighat and Juanes \cite{haghighatSciANNKerasTensorflow2021} introduced SciANN, a 
TensorFlow- and Keras-based 
library developed for scientific computing and physics-informed deep learning.the applications of 
PINNs have expanded rapidly across a wide range of scientific and engineering disciplines. Researchers 
have successfully applied PINNs to nonlinear fluid dynamics, inverse parameter 
identification, computational finance, additive manufacturing, and other data-driven 
scientific computing problems \cite{zhuMachineLearningMetal2020,xuExploreMissingFlow2021,taniosPhysicsInformedNeural2021,savovicComparativeStudyExplicit2023a}. These studies demonstrate the versatility of PINNs 
in solving both forward and inverse PDE problems while effectively incorporating physical 
constraints into the learning process.


In this case, FDM and PINNs are used to find solutions for the one-dimensional time-dependent Schrödinger equation. 
The finite difference method is a traditional numerical approach where the differential operator in 
the equation is approximated at discrete points in a numerical grid. 
The physics-informed neural networks, however, use the loss function, where the PDE is part of the loss 
function and the conditions are added separately, while the neural network attempts to minimize this loss 
function. FDM uses mesh discretization, whereas PINN can produce continuous solutions using automatic 
differentiation without a mesh. The results from both methods will be compared to the analytical 
solution in this paper.



\section{Physics-Informed Neural Networks}


The conventional neural network is trained with the help of data only. If we provide some data samples to 
the network, the network tries to minimize the error between the predicted output values and the observed
 values. Though this methodology has produced very good results in many different scenarios, it needs plenty 
 of data and does not ensure that the solution that is predicted by the model complies with the physical laws.

On the other hand, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) combine the concept of a neural network with the 
physical laws governing the system. Unlike a standard neural network, which is trained using data only, 
a PINN is constrained by physical laws represented by a Partial Differential Equation (PDE). 
Here, the neural network is responsible for approximating the unknown solution $(u(t,x))$, whereas automatic
differentiation is used to calculate the necessary derivatives in the PDE.
For a general PDE of the form

\begin{equation}
u_t + \mathcal{N}[u] = 0
\end{equation}



the neural network predicts the solution $(u(t,x))$. Using automatic differentiation, 
the derivatives $(u_t), (u_x), (u_{xx})$, and higher-order terms can be computed exactly with respect to the network inputs. 
These derivatives are then substituted into the governing equation to form a residual function

\begin{equation}
f(t,x) = u_t + \mathcal{N}[u].
\end{equation}

In case the neural network adheres exactly to the physical law, then the residual 
(f(t,x)) is supposed to take the value of zero throughout the whole domain. 
Hence, there will be two components in the training process of a PINN. 
One is related to the data error while the other component relates to physics, 
which serves as an additional constraint.

A PINN may therefore be regarded as a neural network that makes use of both 
observation data as well as the principles of physics. The incorporation of 
such physical knowledge provides additional information for the network, 
which works as a regularization technique.

    \centering

    \caption{PINN Framework}

\end{figure}


The architecture of a Physics-Informed Neural Network is typically implemented based on a 
fully connected feed-forward neural network, though in many cases they try many other architecture for NN. 
In this structure, the input layer receives the independent variables of 
the problem. For a time-dependent one-dimensional PDE, these inputs are 
usually the spatial coordinate $(x)$ and the time variable $(t)$. 
Therefore, each input point can be shown as $(x,t)$. The role of the 
neural network is mapping this input point to the corresponding approximation of the unknown solution. 
In other words, the network represents the function

\begin{equation}
u_\theta(x,t) \approx u(x,t)
\end{equation}
where $(\theta)$ denotes all trainable parameters of the neural network, including weights and biases.

In the traditional neural network, the output of the architecture is checked only with available data. However, the output from the architecture in a PINN is
further taken to the physics-based part of the algorithm. 
This means that after the network predicts $u_\theta(x,t)$, automatic differentiation is applied to 
compute the derivatives required in the PDE, such as


\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial t},
\frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial x},
\frac{\partial^2 u_\theta}{\partial x^2}.
\end{equation}



To evaluate how well the governing equation is satisfied, 
PINNs introduce a set of collocation points inside the computational domain. 
These points are sampled throughout the domain and are not necessarily 
associated with any observed data. Let the collocation points be denoted by

${(x_f^i,t_f^i)}_{i=1}^{N_f}$

where $(N_f)$ represents the number of collocation points. 
At each of these points, the residual $(f_\theta(x,t))$ is evaluated. If the residual is large at a particular location, it indicates that the neural network 
prediction violates the governing 
equation at that point. Consequently, the network parameters are 
adjusted to reduce this error. One important advantage of this approach is 
that PINNs are mesh-free methods and therefore do not require the computational mesh used by traditional 
numerical techniques such as the Finite Element Method or Finite Difference Method\cite{raissiPhysicsinformedNeuralNetworks2019} \cite{cuomoScientificMachineLearning2022}.


The learning process of a PINN is controlled through a loss function that combines 
information from both the available data and the governing physics. 
In the most general case, the total loss function consists of four components:
$$
\mathcal{L_{\textsl{data}}}
+
\mathcal{L_{\textsl{physics}}}
+
\mathcal{L_{\textsl{boundary}}}
+
\mathcal{L_{\textsl{initial}}}
$$

The data loss measures the difference between the neural network 
predictions and the available observations. The physics loss 
evaluates how well the governing differential equation is 
satisfied at the collocation points. The boundary loss enforces 
the prescribed boundary conditions, while the initial loss 
ensures that the initial conditions are satisfied. Together, 
these terms guide the network toward a physically meaningful solution.
More specifically, the physics loss is often defined as the mean squared value of 
the PDE residual evaluated at all collocation points:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{N_f}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_f}
\left|
f_\theta(x_f^i,t_f^i)
\right|^2
\end{equation}


Minimizing this term forces the neural network to satisfy the 
governing equation throughout the computational domain. Similarly, 
if observational data are available, the data loss can be written as


\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{N_u}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_u}
\left|
u_\theta(x_u^i,t_u^i)-u^i
\right|^2,
\end{equation}

where $(u^i)$ denotes the observed value of the solution at the point $((x_u^i,t_u^i))$.


After the total loss function is constructed, an optimization algorithm such 
as Adam or L-BFGS is used to update the trainable parameters $(\theta)$. 
During each training iteration, the gradients of the loss function with 
respect to the network parameters are computed using backpropagation. 
The optimization process continues until the total loss reaches a sufficiently 
small value, indicating that the neural network simultaneously fits the available 
data and satisfies the governing physical laws.


The Schrödinger equation is the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics and 
describes the evolution of a quantum system through its wave function. In its 
time-dependent form, the equation is written as

\begin{equation}
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}
=
\hat{H}\psi,
\end{equation}

where $\psi(x,t)$ denotes the wave function, $\hbar$ is the reduced 
Planck constant, and $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator. 
The solution of the Schrödinger equation contains complete 
information about the physical system and can be used to 
determine quantities such as probability distributions and energy levels.

The Schrödinger equation plays a central role in many 
scientific fields, including quantum mechanics, 
quantum chemistry, atomic physics, 
condensed matter physics, and quantum computing. 
Because analytical solutions are available only for a 
limited number of simple systems, numerical methods are 
often required to approximate its solutions.

In recent years, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) 
have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional numerical 
approaches. By embedding the governing differential 
equation directly into the loss function, PINNs can learn 
solutions that satisfy both observational data and the 
underlying physical laws. This capability makes PINNs particularly
attractive for solving Schrödinger-type equations, where accurate 
approximations of wave functions are required \cite{wangDatadrivenRogueWaves2021}. 

\subsection{PINN Formulation for the Schrödinger Equation}

In this project, we consider the one-dimensional time-dependent 
Schrödinger equation with a harmonic potential

\begin{equation}
i\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}
=
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2}
+
\frac{1}{2}x^2\psi,
\end{equation}

where $\psi(x,t)$ denotes the complex-valued wave function. The term

\begin{equation}
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2}
\end{equation}

represents the kinetic energy contribution, while

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2}x^2\psi
\end{equation}

corresponds to the harmonic oscillator potential. 
This equation is one of the most important models in 
quantum mechanics and is frequently used as a benchmark 
problem for numerical and machine learning methods.

To solve this problem using PINNs, the neural 
network receives the spatial coordinate 
$x$ and time $t$ as inputs and approximates 
the wave function $\psi(x,t)$. Automatic d
ifferentiation is then employed to compute 
the derivatives required by the governing equation, 
and the residual of the Schrödinger equation is 
incorporated into the loss function. The network 
parameters are optimized such that the predicted 
solution satisfies both the differential equation 
and the prescribed initial and boundary conditions.


To obtain a unique solution of the Schrödinger equation, 
appropriate initial and boundary conditions must be specified.
In this work, the spatial domain is defined as

\begin{equation}
    x \in [-5,5]
\end{equation}


while the temporal domain is chosen as

\begin{equation}
    t \in [0,1]
\end{equation}
The initial condition is selected as a Gaussian wave packet,

\begin{equation}
    \psi(x,0)=e^{-x^2}
\end{equation}

which is a commonly used initial state in quantum mechanics 
due to its smoothness and localized nature. Since the wave 
function is represented in terms of its real and imaginary 
components,

\begin{equation}
    \psi(x,t)=u(x,t)+iv(x,t)
\end{equation}

the initial condition can be written as

\begin{equation}
    u(x,0)=e^{-x^2}
\end{equation}


\begin{equation}
    v(x,0)=0
\end{equation}

These conditions are enforced during training through the initial-condition 
loss term, which penalizes discrepancies between the 
neural network predictions and the prescribed initial values.

Furthermore, homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are 
imposed at the boundaries of the spatial domain. 
Specifically,

\begin{equation}
    u(-5,t)=u(5,t)=0
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
    v(-5,t)=v(5,t)=0
\end{equation}


These boundary conditions ensure that the wave function 
remains negligible at the edges of the computational domain, 
which is consistent with the localized nature of the 
Gaussian initial state.

Within the PINN framework, 
both the initial and boundary conditions are 
incorporated directly into the loss function. 
During training, the neural network is required not 
only to satisfy the governing Schrödinger equation but 
also to reproduce the prescribed initial and boundary 
conditions. This enables the network to learn a physically 
consistent approximation of the wave function throughout 
the spatio-temporal domain.



To solve the time-dependent 


Schrödinger equation using Physics-Informed 
Neural Networks (PINNs), 
the complex-valued wave function is first decomposed into its real and imaginary components. 
Thus, the solution is represented as

\begin{equation}
\psi(x,t)=u(x,t)+i\,v(x,t)
\end{equation}

where $u(x,t)$ and $v(x,t)$ denote the real and imaginary parts of the wave function, respectively.

In the PINN framework, a fully connected neural network is employed to approximate these two functions simultaneously. The inputs of the network are the spatial coordinate $x$ and the time variable $t$, while the outputs correspond to the approximations $u_\theta(x,t)$ and $v_\theta(x,t)$, where $\theta$ denotes the trainable parameters of the neural network.

The governing equation considered in this work is

\begin{equation}
i\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t}
=
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}
+
\frac{1}{2}x^2\psi
\label{eq:schrodinger}
\end{equation}

Substituting

\begin{equation}
\psi=u+i\,v
\end{equation}

into the Schrödinger equation and separating the real and imaginary parts yields the coupled system

\begin{equation}
u_t
=
-\frac{1}{2}v_{xx}
+
V(x)v
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
v_t
=
\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}
-
V(x)u
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^2
\end{equation}

is the harmonic potential.

Using automatic differentiation, the neural network computes the required derivatives $u_t$, $v_t$, $u_{xx}$, and $v_{xx}$. These derivatives are then used to construct the physics residuals

\begin{equation}
f_u
=
u_t
+
\frac{1}{2}v_{xx}
-
V(x)v
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
f_v
=
v_t
-
\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}
+
V(x)u
\end{equation}

If the neural network exactly satisfies the Schrödinger equation, both residual functions 
vanish throughout the computational domain. Therefore, the objective of the PINN is to 
minimize these residuals while simultaneously satisfying the prescribed initial and boundary conditions.

To enforce the governing equation, a set of collocation points is sampled inside the 
spatio-temporal domain. At these points, the residuals $f_u$ and $f_v$ are evaluated 
and incorporated into the loss function. The resulting physics loss is given by

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PDE}}
=
\frac{1}{N_f}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_f}
\left(
|f_u(x_i,t_i)|^2
+
|f_v(x_i,t_i)|^2
\right)
\end{equation}

where $N_f$ denotes the number of collocation points.

The total loss function combines the physics loss with the losses associated with the initial conditions, 
boundary conditions, and available reference data. Therefore,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{PDE}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PDE}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{IC}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IC}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{BC}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{Data}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Data}}
\end{equation}

where the coefficients $\lambda_{\mathrm{PDE}}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{IC}}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{BC}}$, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{Data}}$ control the contribution of each component during training.

\end{figure}




\section{Results and Discussion}

The performance of the proposed physics-informed neural network was evaluated from four complementary perspectives: training 
convergence, agreement with the finite-difference reference solution, satisfaction of the governing 
differential equation, and conservation of the wave-function norm. The results demonstrate that the 
trained network accurately reproduced the spatio-temporal evolution of the wave function while 
maintaining small equation residuals and satisfactory physical consistency throughout the 
computational domain.

The optimization process produced a substantial reduction in all components of the loss 
function. During the first training stage, the Adam optimizer rapidly moved the network 
parameters toward a suitable approximation of the solution. The subsequent L-BFGS stage 
further refined the parameters and reduced the remaining error. This two-stage strategy 
was particularly effective because Adam provided a stable initial approximation, while 
L-BFGS improved the final satisfaction of the physical and observational constraints.

The total loss was defined as the sum of the PDE, boundary-condition, initial-condition, and 
reference-data contributions:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PDE}}
+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}}
+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IC}}
+
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}.
\end{equation}

All four grouped loss components decreased by several orders of magnitude during training. The boundary-condition loss reached the smallest final value, indicating that the homogeneous boundary conditions were satisfied with high accuracy. The PDE loss remained the largest component at the end of optimization because the governing equation had to be enforced throughout the entire space-time domain rather than only along the boundaries or initial line. The total training and testing losses followed nearly identical convergence patterns, indicating consistent performance across the sampled training and testing points. This agreement, however, should be interpreted as consistency on the sampled sets rather than definitive proof that overfitting is impossible.

The accuracy of the trained network was further evaluated by comparing its predicted probability density with the reference solution generated using the finite-difference method. The probability density was calculated from the real and imaginary components as

\begin{equation}
\rho(x,t)
=
|\psi(x,t)|^2
=
u(x,t)^2+v(x,t)^2.
\end{equation}

Close agreement was obtained between the PINN and FDM probability-density fields throughout the entire computational domain. Both methods produced the same overall spatio-temporal structure, including the central concentration of the wave packet and its gradual broadening over the simulated time interval. No visible displacement or distortion of the principal density structure was observed in the PINN prediction.

The pointwise error was calculated using

\begin{equation}
E_{\rho}(x,t)
=
\left|
\rho_{\mathrm{PINN}}(x,t)
-
\rho_{\mathrm{FDM}}(x,t)
\right|.
\end{equation}

The largest errors were concentrated near the central region, where the probability density was greatest and changed most rapidly in space and time. Much smaller errors occurred toward the boundaries because the wave-function magnitude approached zero in those regions. This spatial distribution is reasonable because reproducing the height, width, and temporal evolution of the central wave packet is more difficult than approximating nearly zero values far from its centre.

The error of the complete complex wave function is consequently a more representative

The full-field comparison was complemented by examining the probability-density profiles at representative time instants. This comparison provides a more detailed view of whether the network reproduced the local peak, width, symmetry, and spatial decay of the wave packet.

At \(t=0\), the predicted density closely matched the prescribed initial profile. As time progressed, the central peak gradually decreased while the distribution became wider. This behaviour indicates a redistribution of the wave-function density over a broader spatial region. The reduction in peak height does not represent a loss of norm because the area under the density profile remained approximately constant.

The PINN and FDM curves overlapped closely at

\[
t=0,\quad 0.25,\quad 0.50,\quad 0.75,\quad 1.00.

The agreement was maintained not only near the maximum density but also along the tails of the distribution. Satisfaction of the governing equation was investigated by evaluating the two coupled residuals produced by the real-valued formulation of the Schrödinger equation:

\begin{align}
f_u(x,t)
&=
u_t+\frac{1}{2}v_{xx}-V(x)v, \\
f_v(x,t)
&=
v_t-\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}+V(x)u.
\end{align}

For an exact solution, both residuals would be zero throughout the 
computational domain. The trained PINN produced residuals that remained 
small over most of the space-time region. Their magnitudes generally 
occurred around \(10^{-3}\) or below, although several localized regions 
reached values of several \(10^{-3}\).

The residual distributions contained curved low-value bands where 
the signed residuals passed through zero. These structures do not represent discontinuities in the wave function. They result from plotting the absolute residual magnitude, which becomes very small wherever the signed residual changes sign.

A logarithmic colour scale was used because the residual values covered several orders of magnitude. The resulting distributions of \(|f_u|\) and \(|f_v|\) are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pde_residuals}.

    colour scale is used to display the residual magnitudes.}

real and imaginary equations, respectively. Their RMS values were approximately \(1.0\times10^{-3}\), 
whereas the maximum residuals were \(7.4639\times10^{-3}\) and \(6.7442\times10^{-3}\).

These values are consistent with the final PDE loss. Since the PDE loss is based on squared 
residuals, an RMS residual of approximately \(10^{-3}\) produces a squared contribution of 
approximately \(10^{-6}\). The numerical agreement between the residual statistics and the 
loss history confirms that the two diagnostics are internally consistent.

The maximum values were approximately seven times greater than the corresponding RMS

An additional assessment of physical consistency was performed 
by calculating the integrated probability density:

\begin{equation}
N(t)
=
\int_{-5}^{5}
|\psi(x,t)|^2\,dx.
\end{equation}

For Schrödinger evolution under a real potential, this quantity 
should remain constant in time. The finite-difference reference 
solution preserved the norm almost exactly, producing a horizontal 
curve over the full temporal interval. This behaviour is expected 
because the matrix-exponential time evolution used by the reference 
solver preserves the discrete wave-function norm to numerical precision.

The PINN result was also nearly constant but displayed small 
oscillations around the reference level. These fluctuations arise 
because the neural network satisfies the governing equation and 
constraints approximately rather than exactly. Importantly, no 
systematic increasing or decreasing trend was observed, indicating 
that the model did not experience substantial accumulated norm drift.

The initial norm was approximately \(1.2533\), rather than unity. Therefore, the plotted quantity should be described as the wave-function norm rather than total probability. This value indicates that the initial wave function used in the experiment was not normalized to one. Although the lack of normalization does not affect the comparison between the PINN and FDM solutions, normalization would be preferable for a standard quantum-mechanical interpretation.

The combined numerical evidence demonstrates that the proposed PINN 
accurately approximated the solution of the time-dependent Schrödinger 
equation over the considered domain. Stable convergence was achieved 
through the hybrid Adam and L-BFGS optimization procedure, resulting 
in a final loss of \(2.4256\times10^{-6}\). The boundary and initial 
conditions were satisfied accurately, while the PDE residual formed 
the largest remaining contribution to the loss.

Strong agreement was obtained between the PINN and finite-difference 
solutions at both the field and profile levels. The relative \(L_2\) error of the c
omplete wave function was approximately \(0.155\%\), while the corresponding density 
error was approximately \(0.097\%\). The maximum pointwise density error remained 
limited to \(1.4054\times10^{-3}\), and the much smaller MAE and RMSE values showed 
that this maximum discrepancy occurred only in localized regions.

The residual analysis confirmed that the small prediction error was 
accompanied by satisfactory enforcement of the governing equation. Both real and imaginary residuals 
had RMS magnitudes of approximately \(10^{-3}\), which was consistent with their squared 
loss contributions of approximately \(10^{-6}\). The close similarity between the residual 
statistics of the two equations further indicated that neither component of the coupled 
system was substantially less well represented.

Physical consistency was also maintained throughout the simulation. The PINN preserved the 
wave-function norm with a maximum relative deviation of only \(0.0237\%\). Although this 
level of conservation was less exact than that of the finite-difference reference method, 
it remained sufficiently small to confirm that the predicted evolution did not suffer from 
significant artificial gain or loss of norm.

Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. The present model was evaluated 
for one potential, one temporal interval, and one network configuration. In addition, reference-data 
constraints were included during training, meaning that the proposed model represents a 
data-assisted PINN rather than a completely data-free formulation. Further experiments should 
therefore examine the performance of the model without reference data, with different numbers of 
collocation points, and with alternative network depths and widths. Longer temporal intervals 
and more complicated potentials would also provide a stronger assessment of generalization.

The initial wave function should be normalized in future experiments so that the conserved 
integral equals one and can be interpreted directly as total probability. An ablation study 
comparing Adam-only training with the hybrid Adam--L-BFGS procedure would additionally clarify 
the contribution of the second optimization stage. Despite these limitations, the current 
results provide strong evidence that the proposed PINN formulation can accurately reproduce 
the dynamics of the investigated Schrödinger system while satisfying the governing equation
and preserving its principal integral property.



\section{Conclusion}

In this study, a physics-informed neural network was developed to solve the one-dimensional 
time-dependent Schrödinger equation with a harmonic potential, and its predictions were 
compared with a finite-difference reference solution. The numerical results showed a close 
agreement between the two approaches over the entire space-time domain. The PINN reproduced 
the evolution of the wave function and probability density with small relative errors, while 
the low PDE residuals confirmed that the governing equation was satisfied accurately. The 
model also preserved the wave-function norm with only a small deviation, although the finite-difference 
method achieved conservation to near machine precision.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the proposed data-assisted PINN is a reliable approach for 
approximating the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. While the finite-difference method remains 
highly accurate for the structured one-dimensional problem considered here, the PINN provides a 
continuous and flexible approximation that can be evaluated at arbitrary points without constructing a 
new spatial grid. The ideas presented in this work may therefore support the further development of 
physics-informed models for more complex quantum systems, including problems with time-dependent 
potentials, longer simulation intervals, and higher-dimensional domains. Future studies should also 
examine purely physics-informed formulations without reference data and investigate the influence of 
network architecture, collocation-point distribution, and optimization strategy on the accuracy and 
stability of the solution.
